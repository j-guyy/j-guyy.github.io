The Adventure

I put together a touring route with two non-negotiable stops: Zoagli, the little Ligurian town my great-grandfather left for the US in 1914, and Torricella Peligna, the Abruzzo mountain town Monica's great-grandparents left in 1928. Everything in between was up to us: down the Ligurian coast, through the hills of Tuscany and the valleys of Umbria, over the Apennines, and out to the Adriatic at Pescara.

We rode our Trek Checkpoints with rear racks and two 20L panniers each. Water is everywhere in Italy, so we only carried 2.5L at a time, and fueling was simple with all the delicious food along the way (plus a lot of baby food). The tour came in four legs: Ligurian Coast, Tuscany Hills, Umbria Valleys, and Abruzzo & Apennines.

Getting to the start line was an adventure of its own. Traveling with bikes is hard: we missed our train out of Milano Centrale by mere seconds and spent the next hour and a half camped out on the platform with our bike bags.

Day 1 — Genoa to Zoagli

We slept in until 8:30, then rose in a shock: the bike bags had to ship before checkout. We hobbled through Genoa with those jumbo, inconveniently-dimensioned bags, took the metro to Piazza de Ferrari and struggled our way to Mail Boxes Etc. Our hero, Michael of MBE, called our final hotel and yelled at them in Italian for a solid five minutes, then promised us they would hold the bags until we arrived in two weeks. He also said it's Rome, and people don't really do their jobs there. We'll see how that goes.

Back at the hotel, the rear rack on my bike was missing a bolt. The first bike shop, supposed to open at 9am, was closed. The second, supposed to open at 10:30, was also closed. So I moved some mounted components around to scrappily hold the rack in place, pumped up our tires, and it was time to begin.

Within minutes of rolling into Genoa, we watched a moped driver honk at a cyclist, the cyclist scream back in Italian, and the moped driver spit on him. "Welcome at Genova," he told us as he wiped his face. Nasty. After a late smoothie-and-salad breakfast we headed south along the coast: views in Sori, a climb up Ruta di Camogli, a descent behind the world's slowest moped driver (she yelled at us when we passed), a detour out the peninsula to Portofino, poke bowls in Rapallo, and finally our first stop, Zoagli. My great-grandfather walked these same streets before he moved to the US in 1914.

Day 2 — Zoagli to Levanto

A sunny walk along the coastal path in Zoagli, then straight into a big climb with no warm-up. At least the high was 82 instead of yesterday's 91. In Chiavari we found The Bicycle Shop & Service, where nobody spoke English, so I explained the missing rack bolt in hand signals. After 30 minutes of three Italian men yelling at each other and trying things, the rack was back on and we were smooth sailing.

Sestri Levante was beautiful, with palm trees and colorful buildings, and lunch at Gusto Sano (a delicious rice and roasted veggie bowl plus tiny acai bowls) ended up as one of the best meals of the trip. Then came the big climb over Passo del Bracco. Monica slid off the road on the uphill while putting a bottle away and scraped up her knee and palms. Medkit out, bandages on, and we kept going. Near the top the flowering trees and lush lowland greens gave way to pines, with the transition right around 2,000 ft. We bombed down the windy coastal roads into Levanto, floated in the waves at Spiaggia Levanto, and had my first pasta of Italy at Da Tapulin, with cuculli bread and a sunset on the jetty.

Day 3 — Cinque Terre, then on to Sarzana

Breakfast at the B&B was a tower of fruit, bread, brownies and eggs. A couple from Firenze heard we were biking to Pescara and told us we had "bono gambe": strong legs.

We left the bikes at the B&B and took the train into Cinque Terre, starting at the far end with Riomaggiore and its hilltop castle. We hiked over to Manarola, which was very sun-exposed and hot. The heat and humidity were like Florida, but with mountains instead of swamps. Manarola's viewpoint ended up being my favorite of the five. Corniglia sits up a big staircase, which kept the crowds small and got us an easy table for salads and pinsa. In Vernazza, the most photographed of the five, Google Maps led us astray, so I used the Strava global heatmap to find the viewpoint. Monterosso was a quick stop for the big beach and the Neptune statue. The 35 euro Cinque Terre pass seemed steep at first, but it covered trails, bathrooms and the train, so it was worth SOME value.

Back in Levanto we changed into bike clothes beside the house and knocked out some of tomorrow's miles: one big hill of about 1,800 ft with steep grades back to back, then a descent to Pignone, where an older man yelled something in Italian that ended, pointed at each of us in turn, with "DIE! DIE!" He must not like cyclists. We rolled through small towns with ever-improving views of the Apuan Alps, reached Sarzana around sunset, demolished 24 plates at an all-you-can-eat sushi place, and found our B&B through some dark, semi-sketchy neighborhoods at 9:45pm.

Day 4 — Sarzana to Lucca

Croissants, grapefruit and lactose-free yogurt, then a grocery stop for snacks and Liquid IV, coffee and a Torta Pasqualina at a tiny café, and a look at the Fortezza Firmafede on the way out. Viale 25 Aprile took us to the coast at a perfect -1% grade with tons of other cyclists. Marinella beach had unreal views of the Apuan Alps and reminded me of Salobreña.

From Marina di Massa we turned inland toward the heart of Tuscany. The only thing open in Camaiore was a grungy bar full of old dudes who loved staring at Monica, so we ate our croissants fast and got out of there. We booked a hotel on the descent and dropped into the walled city of Lucca around 3pm. Salmon spaghetti and a massive salad with suuuper fresh olives at Trattoria Da Rosolo, then a walk around the city on top of the wall, past the Piazza dell'Anfiteatro and the Basilica di San Frediano. The only bummer: we couldn't buy gifts yet, since we'd have to carry them the rest of the way.

Day 5 — Lucca to San Gimignano

We solved the gift dilemma by the end of the day. Read on.

At Chronò Bikes the owner found us some pedal straps for hanging laundry and asked whether we were going north or south. Odd question, until I remembered that Lucca sits on the Via Francigena, the old pilgrim route, which is split into a northern and a southern way. In Altopascio, famous for the bread it baked for pilgrims, we bought a loaf bigger than Monica's head. The bread itself wasn't that good, but it was worth it for the experience, and we snacked on it all day. Over the Ponte Mediceo into Fucecchio, a coffee stop, and a man who told us the best part of the Via Francigena is south of Siena. Let's see if he's right.

Then the rolling hills began: up to San Miniato for big views of Tuscany, and one climb after another to Castelfiorentino, with drivers loving to ZOOM on the back roads. SR429 to Certaldo was terrible, with fast trucks and no shoulder. Then we hit our first roadblock of the trip, literally: a closed road, a private-property detour that didn't work, and a highway workaround. It only added 2–3 miles, but man was that annoying. The final climb through vineyards and olive groves brought us into San Gimignano.

That night we bought wine and olive oil and had it shipped straight back to the US. No need to carry bottles on our bikes. There were two dinners, and we walked out of one restaurant after being seated when we realized it was the wrong place. Dinner #2 was chicken and gnocchi at Giardino Bistrot, where Monica declared the local Vernaccia the best wine of her life, hands down.

Day 6 — San Gimignano to Siena

Our first gravel of the trip took us down from San Gimignano to Colle di Val d'Elsa. We stashed our panniers in the bushes and walked the Sentierelsa trail in bike shoes to see the Elsa river, which was a bright glacial blue, the kind you'd expect in a Canadian valley and not in central Tuscany. The Via Francigena climbs to the walled town of Monteriggioni on a gravel road that hits 19.8%, so we walked the bikes up. No reason to burn ourselves out for a side quest.

We reached Siena early, and Monica was hangry. Actually quite hangry. ("How many more minutes?" "Are we on the street yet?") Smoothies and plates of pasta at La Fontana della Frutta fixed that. Then came a lift in the hotel gym and a laundry odyssey: the first laundromat had bad reviews and the second was closed when it was supposed to be open, but the third had dry, great-smelling clothes. It was like starting the trip over. In the Piazza del Campo a marching band paraded seashell flags: Siena is split into 17 contrade, medieval neighborhood factions that are still rivals today. Pici and salmon at Vivace was the best meal of the trip so far, and over dinner we booked the last available room in Torricella Peligna for later in the trip. Phew.

Day 7 — Siena to Pienza

Bells rang all over the city at 7:30am. After a great hotel breakfast and a grocery run (Moonvalley oat and date bars, the perfect fuel, plus more baby food, which is rocket fuel), we dropped out of Siena onto the first of five planned gravel roads.

About a mile into Gravel 1, Monica turned to check for a car on an uphill, slid into a bump and crashed, cutting up the same leg and palm as her first crash. I patched her up under the only shade tree on the trail, using our last alcohol wipe. Her head hurt a bit, which made me want to bail, but she insisted on continuing. I made her agree, on video, that if she crashed again the ride was over and we'd catch a train to Pienza.

Gravels 2 and 3 were friendly rollers and a long downhill to Buonconvento. Gravel 4 was TOUGH: punchy climbs at 13% and then 16%, sliding on loose gravel, with my front wheel lifting on the second. We refueled on pastries at Ticci Bar in Torrenieri, then took a surprise gravel descent to the Antiche Terme Romane Libere, free Roman thermal pools full of mud, where we dipped our legs in the water. Gravel 5 was steady with Pienza in sight at the top of the hill. On the final paved climb I got ahead, waited, and watched an ambulance race down past me. I was convinced something had happened. Then a bright blue bike with reflective yellow panniers crested the horizon. She was okay.

Dinner in Pienza went to plan B (the first restaurant was closed for a private event) and then plan C. The pici was delicious, my first bruschetta just tasted like toast with olive oil, and second dinner was smoked salmon avocado toast at a bistro next to our B&B.

Day 8 — Pienza to Orvieto

Our last morning in Tuscany started with breakfast in Montepulciano. Monica's rear derailleur had been knocked out of line, so we stopped at a bike shop in Ciconia to realign it, then rolled 56 miles and nearly 4,000 ft into Umbria. Orvieto sits on top of a cliff of volcanic rock, so of course the day ended with a climb. That night we went down into the network of caves and tunnels carved into the rock beneath the town.

Day 9 — Orvieto to Spoleto

The heat was miserable, topping out at 98°F. We stopped in Todi halfway to refuel and cool off, then ground out the rest of the 53 miles and 3,700 ft to Spoleto.

Day 10 — Assisi side quest, then Spoleto to Norcia

With a short ride on the schedule, Monica had a last-minute idea: leave the panniers, zoom to the Spoleto train station, and spend the morning in Assisi. The bikes felt great without the weight. Assisi was a really beautiful town and a perfect way to spend the hottest part of the day. Then we took the train back to Spoleto, loaded up, and rode into the mountains to Norcia.

Day 11 — Norcia to L'Aquila

The longest day of the tour: 61 miles and 4,446 ft over the Apennines, cresting 1,282 m (4,206 ft), and out of Umbria into Abruzzo. We finished in L'Aquila, the capital of Abruzzo.

Day 12 — L'Aquila to Sulmona

A gentler 46 miles down to Sulmona: mountains, aqueducts, and chilling with Ovid, the town's most famous son.

Day 13 — Sulmona to Torricella Peligna

The hardest day of climbing on the whole trip: 4,995 ft in 39 miles, topping out at 1,393 m (4,570 ft), with the reward at the end. We made it to Torricella Peligna, the town Monica's great-grandparents left for the US in 1928. That evening we stopped at the cemetery to visit her ancestors.

Day 14 — Il Finale: Torricella Peligna to Pescara

We spent the morning in Torricella, with a farmers market and a mountain viewpoint. Monica cried.

Then came Il Finale: 47 miles, mostly downhill, out of the mountains to the Adriatic at Pescara.

Genoa to Pescara: 597 miles, 14 riding days, and both of our great-grandparents' hometowns. What a trip.

The next morning we rode to the station and took the train to Rome with our bikes, then spent the afternoon riding out to the Roman aqueducts on the edge of the city. And the bike bags? Thankfully they were waiting for us at our Best Western in downtown Rome. Michael's five minutes of yelling paid off.
